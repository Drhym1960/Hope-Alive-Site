## Summary

For a child growing up in poverty or without parental support, access to education is rarely guaranteed. School fees, uniforms, textbooks, and the simple ability to attend regularly can all become obstacles. When those obstacles are removed, something remarkable often happens — children discover capability, confidence, and a sense of their own future. This article explores why education support is one of the most impactful ways to help vulnerable children.

## The Obstacles to Education for Vulnerable Children

Understanding the barriers to education helps explain why targeted support is necessary.

**Cost** is the most immediate obstacle for many families and caregivers. School fees, uniforms, shoes, and learning materials add up quickly. For a family living close to the poverty line, these costs can be the difference between a child attending school and staying home.

**Instability** is another significant barrier. Children who have experienced loss, displacement, or trauma may struggle to attend school consistently. Without a stable home environment and consistent support, even children who are enrolled may fall behind or drop out.

**Hunger** affects concentration and learning. A child who arrives at school without having eaten, or who is hungry by midday, cannot focus as effectively as one who is well-nourished. This is one reason why feeding programmes and education support are most powerful when they work together.

**Social exclusion** can mean that children from vulnerable backgrounds face stigma that makes school a difficult or unwelcoming environment. Mentorship and psychosocial support can help address this.

## What Education Support Looks Like

Education support for vulnerable children encompasses more than paying school fees. A comprehensive approach includes:

- **School fees and enrolment costs** — ensuring children can register and remain enrolled
- **Uniforms, shoes, and bags** — removing a visible source of stigma and ensuring children are equipped to attend
- **Textbooks, exercise books, and stationery** — the materials children need to learn
- **After-school tutoring and homework support** — helping children who have fallen behind catch up
- **Mentorship** — older students or community members investing time in younger children's development
- **Support for transitions** — particularly the move from primary to secondary school, which is a common point at which vulnerable children drop out

## Education as Protection

Education does more than prepare children for employment — it protects them. Children who are in school are less exposed to the risks of street life, child labour, and exploitation. A school day provides structure, supervision, and access to trusted adults. The relationships formed at school — with teachers, peers, and mentors — can be a source of stability for children who have experienced significant loss.

For girls specifically, education provides critical protection. Girls who remain in school are less likely to experience early or forced marriage, and more likely to make informed decisions about their own health and futures.

## The Long-Term Impact of Education

The benefits of education for vulnerable children extend well beyond their individual lives. A child who completes their education and gains employment becomes a contributing member of their community. They are more likely to support their own children's education. They are more likely to be engaged in civic life. They are less likely to experience poverty as adults.

This multiplier effect is one reason why education support is considered one of the highest-impact interventions available. The change that begins with a school uniform and a set of textbooks can reach across generations.

There are several ways to support education access for children in need:

## Education Is a Right, Not a Privilege

Every child deserves an education. This is not a controversial statement — it is enshrined in international conventions on the rights of the child. But rights without resources are hollow. For children living in poverty, without parental support, or in unstable circumstances, the gap between the right to education and the reality of accessing it can feel unbridgeable.

That is why organisations like HACS Foundation exist — not to replace the systems that should protect children, but to bridge the gaps that those systems have not yet closed.
